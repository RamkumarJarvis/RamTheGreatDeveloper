# Nobody sells a home try-on mirror yet

No product on sale in October 2026 does what you describe: a full-length home mirror with a touch surface that lists your own clothes and shows you wearing a chosen outfit live as you move. The nearest are Korea's HEYMIRROR, a CES 2026 award honoree that stores a household's wardrobe and shows AI-generated outfit previews but has no confirmed camera or live try-on, and LOOOK.AI's shop mirrors, which since March 2026 have dressed moving shoppers in catalogue clothes using a real-time video model that one analysis prices at US$0.05 per second. Two limits explain the gap: optics rules out painting convincing clothes onto a true reflection, so every working try-on mirror is really a camera feeding a screen, and photoreal live rendering of arbitrary garments costs about ₹1,300 per five minutes in the cloud and cannot run on a home AI board. The buildable version is a "digital mirror": a portrait 55-inch screen behind high-transmission two-way glass, a wide-angle camera, and a Jetson-class board that tracks your body live and draws a lower-fidelity preview of your own garment on you, while a cloud model returns a photoreal still 5–20 seconds later. A DIY prototype costs roughly ₹0.9–1.7 lakh (US$1,000–1,900) and a mass-produced unit roughly US$940–1,590 before margin and GST, which puts retail above ₹1 lakh. Shock safety is an architecture decision: keep 230 V inside certified, sealed power supplies and run everything a hand can reach at 24 V DC or less, the "ES1" class that India's newly adopted IS/IEC 62368-1:2023 lets ordinary people touch. The largest risk is the market, not the electronics — Amazon's Echo Look, Lululemon's MIRROR, Naked Labs and Google's Doppl all shut down, and Google already offers free photo try-on in India — so build the software on a laptop first, then a phone app, then run home or showroom pilots, and make custom hardware last.

The exceptions are the AI-model licences, model speeds and cloud-GPU prices, which were read directly from the projects' GitHub files, model cards and a published price catalogue. Figures marked **(est.)** are calculations or assumptions made for this report, not quoted prices. Rupee–dollar conversions use an assumed ₹88 per US$, which no cited source supplies. Where sources conflict or rest on a single vendor, the text says so.

## No product pairs your own clothes with live try-on

Mirror-plus-camera try-on is fifteen years old, but it has always dressed shoppers in a retailer's catalogue, never in their own wardrobe. The first wave (2011–2015) used Kinect or RealSense depth cameras: Cisco's StyleMe drew 1,402 shoppers during its John Lewis pilot ([Cisco case study](https://www3-realm.cisco.com/web/about/ac79/docs/retail/John-Lewis-and-Cisco-Virtual-fashion-mirror-case-study.pdf)), FaceCake's Swivel ran a ten-day test at Bloomingdale's ([Sixteen:Nine](https://www.sixteen-nine.net/?p=4907)), and Korea's FXMirror reportedly cost one Singapore bridal shop **S$45,000** ([Vulcan Post](https://vulcanpost.com/?p=550031)). A second wave of "smart fitting rooms" recorded video and read RFID tags rather than drawing clothes, and its best-known startups were absorbed: MemoMi by Walmart in 2022 ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/memomi-labs)) and Oak Labs by the kiosk maker ZIVELO in 2018 ([AV Network](https://www.avnetwork.com/news/zivelo-acquires-oak-labs)). The current wave generates garments from ordinary product photos, which removes the old cost of 3D-modelling every item. INDE's Vyu AI (August 2025) emails you a still photo of yourself in the outfit ([Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/press-release/20250822-vyu-ai-photo-real-ai-clothing-try-on-mirror-goes-live-in-north-america-1)), and LOOOK.AI (March 2026) renders live, movement-following try-on through Decart's real-time video model, with the shopper steering it from a phone after scanning a QR code ([Retail Technology Innovation Hub](https://retailtechinnovationhub.com/home/2026/3/19/loookai-goes-live-with-real-time-ai-powered-clothing-try-on-feature-for-smart-mirrors)). Snap shut its AR enterprise unit, AR Mirrors included, about six months after launching it in 2023 ([Retail TouchPoints](https://www.retailtouchpoints.com/news/citing-rise-of-generative-ai-snap-nixes-ares-white-label-ar-offering/137870/)). The one system found that worked on clothes the shopper was actually wearing was Uniqlo's 2012 Magic Mirror, which recoloured a jacket on the wearer in real time ([UrbanDaddy](https://www.urbandaddy.com/articles/20328/san-francisco/uniqlo-qlo-in-the-dark-a-japanese-megastore-lands-in-union-square)).

At home, **no device has ever shipped virtual clothing try-on**. Amazon's Echo Look (US$199.99 in 2017) photographed your own outfits and rated them, with no mirror and no try-on, and stopped working on 24 July 2020 after Amazon moved its style features into the Shopping app ([Android Authority](https://androidauthority.com/amazon-echo-look-camera-767386); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/05/29/echo-looks-will-cease-functioning-in-july-as-amazon-discontinues-the-camera)). The home smart mirrors that did ship, from HiMirror to Samsung's CES 2025 beauty mirror, analyse skin, health or mood. In India, Hyderabad's Dharpan.ai installs six-foot smart mirrors in shops ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2025/05/dharpanais-smart-mirrors-are-changing-how-india-shops)), TCS demonstrated a store try-on mirror in February 2026 ([ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/technology/ai-impact-summit-2026-smart-mirror-by-tcs-promises-to-transform-retail-shopping-experience-enn26021902639)), and Qualcomm showed a mirror at India Mobile Congress (7–10 October 2026) that runs a 7-billion-parameter vision-language model offline to describe your outfit and suggest improvements, with no try-on ([TelecomTalk](https://telecomtalk.info/ai-mirror-analyses-your-outfit-in-time/1012703/)). The researchers found no display mirror with try-on on Amazon.in or Flipkart and no home try-on mirror on Kickstarter or Indiegogo. The table lists the nearest neighbours.

## Real reflections cannot wear virtual clothes, so build a digital mirror

The obvious design, a display behind two-way glass that paints clothes onto your reflection, fails for three physical reasons. First, focus: your reflection sits as far behind the glass as you stand in front of it, so it is **twice as far from your eyes** as anything drawn on the display ([USPTO patent 11109015](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11109015)). Researchers call this the "fixed focal length problem", in which the eyes must keep refocusing or see one layer blurred ([MERL](https://www.merl.com/publications/docs/TR2006-105.pdf)), and a 2023 study found that laying a half-mirror over a screen "leads to depth mismatch" ([3D-Mirrorcle](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.13617)). The mismatch is about 1 dioptre at 0.5 m and 0.25 dioptre at 2 m (est.), worst exactly when you lean in to inspect a collar. Second, two eyes: the lines of sight from your left and right eyes to the same point on your reflected body cross the glass about **31.5 mm apart at any distance**, half a typical 63 mm eye spacing, so an overlay lines up for one eye and looks doubled to the other (est.). Fixing that needs separate images for each eye plus eye tracking, still research-grade, which is why Amazon's 2018 mirror patent had cameras locate the viewer's face and eyes ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2018/amazon-patents-blended-reality-mirror-shows-wearing-virtual-clothes-virtual-locales/)). Third, light only adds: a display behind half-silvered glass can add light but never darken ([W3C archive](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webapps-github/2021Jan/0735.html)), so a dark virtual kurta drawn over a white real shirt appears as a faint tint on white.

Commercial systems therefore film you and show video. MemoMi's MemoryMirror used a roughly 70-inch screen with a camera on top and corrected the perspective in real time because, its CEO said, the raw image "would look totally distorted" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/alltechconsidered/2015/04/10/398811812/magic-mirror-at-the-store-should-this-top-go-in-my-drawer)). Your build should work the same way. The two-way glass becomes a styling layer that acts as an ordinary mirror while the screen is dark; during try-on the screen shows a flipped, corrected video of you with the garment composited in, and it must outshine your real reflection, which would otherwise show through as a ghost.

How bright the screen must be depends on the glass. The table converts each option into the panel brightness needed for the screen image to be five times brighter than the reflection of mid-tone clothing in a 500-lux room (est., using luminance = reflectance × illuminance ÷ π ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminance)) with assumed room-light and fabric values).

| Glass | Reflects / transmits | Panel brightness for 5:1 (est.) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pilkington MirroView, standard | ~66–74% / ~20–22% ([Glass Online](https://www.glassonline.com/interactive-mirrors-with-pilkington-mirroview/); [Pilkington bulletin](https://www.pilkington.com/-/media/pilkington/site-content/usa/window-manufacturers/technical-bulletins/ats191mirroviewdesignfabinstall20130107.pdf)) | ~1,060 nits | A 400-nit TV loses even to the reflection of a white shirt |
| MirroView 50/50, 6 mm only | ~50% / ~37% ([Pilkington bulletin](https://www.pilkington.com/~/media/Pilkington/Site%20Content/USA/Architects/ATS%20191%20MirroView%20Design%20Fab%20Install%202015%2004%2017%20NSG%20format.pdf)) | ~430 nits | Works with a bright TV and dimmed room lights |
| Acrylic two-way sheet | ~70% / ~30% ([TwoWayMirrors](https://www.twowaymirrors.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/acrylic-two-way-mirror.pdf)) | ~740 nits | Light and shatter-resistant, but flexes and distorts at full length |
| "Dielectric" bright-room glass | ~40% / ~60% ([MagicMirror forum](https://forum.magicmirror.builders/post/132)) | ~210 nits | Easiest for try-on; a dimmer mirror |
| Reflective-polariser film (Samsung ML55E type) | ~55% / ~90% of LCD light ([B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1266767-REG/samsung_ml55e_55_full_hd.html)) | ~195 nits | Best trade-off on paper; no Indian supplier found |

Choose MirroView 50/50 or a 40/60 dielectric glass and allow roughly twice the panel brightness in the table, because mid-tone garments are drawn well below the panel's peak white (est.). Two conflicts follow. The bright, colour-accurate front lighting you need for judging colours also brightens the reflection the screen must beat, so the mirror's own lights must dim when try-on starts. And high-transmission glass makes a dimmer mirror that shows the LCD's grey "black" glow in a dark room; builders report the glow stays invisible only while the room in front is brighter than the monitor behind ([MagicMirror forum](https://forum.magicmirror.builders/post/45961)).

Size follows from a school-physics rule: a flat mirror needs to be only **half your height** to show your whole body, at any distance ([University of Illinois Physics Van](https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/1936)). Covering users from 160 to 190 cm needs a band from about 0.74 m to 1.84 m above the floor (est.). A 55-inch panel in portrait is 121.8 cm tall, so with its bottom edge about 0.65 m up it covers everyone in that range; a 50-inch panel (110.7 cm) just covers it, and a 43-inch panel (95.2 cm) works only if software shifts the image (est.). The software must also behave like glass: flip the picture left to right, as MediaPipe's sample code does for a "selfie-view display" ([MediaPipe](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/blob/master/docs/solutions/pose.md)), draw your body at half its real height, and rescale it as you step closer or back.

The camera is the last optical choice. Framing a 2-metre-tall zone from 1.5 m away takes about **67° of vertical field of view** from a camera at mid-height, 53° from 2 m, or about 55° from the top bezel tilted 24° down, so mount the camera rotated 90° to put its wide axis vertical (est.). A top-bezel camera is simple but looks down at you, so the image needs MemoMi-style perspective correction. A camera behind the glass at eye level gives the truest mirror view but loses most of its light in the coating: one supplier says its 11%-transparency glass "blocks almost all of the light" and recommends a shroud ([TwoWayMirrors](https://www.twowaymirrors.com/hide-camera-behind-mirror/)), and builders report that some coatings work with a camera behind them and some do not ([MagicMirror forum](https://forum.magicmirror.builders/topic/2143/using-the-raspberry-pi-camera-to-see-through-the-mirror/19?page=1)). Start on the top bezel and move behind the glass in a later version.

## Live tracking is solved; live photoreal clothing is not

Following your movements is the easy half. Google's MediaPipe BlazePose returns 33 3D body landmarks and a body mask in 25 ms per frame on the GPU of a 2018 Pixel 3 phone ([MediaPipe](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/blob/master/docs/solutions/pose.md)), and MediaPipe's Apache-licensed multiclass segmenter labels each pixel as background, hair, skin or **clothes**, though its model card warns that masks are not pixel-perfect and degrade in low light, backlight and fast motion ([MediaPipe model card](https://storage.googleapis.com/mediapipe-assets/Model%20Card%20Multiclass%20Segmentation.pdf)). The boards that fit inside a mirror all keep up. One RK3588 NPU core runs a YOLOv8n pose model at 58 fps ([RKNN Model Zoo](https://github.com/airockchip/rknn_model_zoo)); a Hailo-8L runs YOLOv8s-pose at 107 fps on an x86 test host, so expect less on a Pi 5 ([Hailo Model Zoo](https://github.com/hailo-ai/hailo_model_zoo/blob/master/docs/public_models/HAILO8L/HAILO8L_pose_estimation.rst)); and a Jetson Orin Nano Super runs YOLO26n detection in 4.57 ms ([Ultralytics](https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/blob/main/docs/en/guides/nvidia-jetson.md)). The Apache-licensed RTMPose-s takes 1.39 ms on a GTX 1660 Ti ([RTMPose](https://github.com/open-mmlab/mmpose/tree/main/projects/rtmpose)). Tracking a body at 30 fps is a solved problem with headroom to spare.

Drawing *your* garment on that moving skeleton is the hard half, and every route has a catch. Full 3D cloth simulation gives the steadiest result but needs a simulation-ready 3D garment for every item, which suits a retailer's catalogue and not a personal wardrobe; the standard SMPL-X body model is also licensed for non-commercial research only ([SMPL-X licence](https://github.com/vchoutas/smplx/blob/main/LICENSE)), though Meta's MHR body model is Apache-2.0 ([MHR](https://github.com/facebookresearch/MHR)). Learned 2D warping is fast, with DM-VTON reaching 40 fps on a T4 GPU in 37 MB of memory, but that model is licensed for academic use only ([DM-VTON](https://github.com/KiseKloset/DM-VTON)). Snap's Garment Transfer does live upper-body try-on from one 2D garment image ([Snap](https://developers.snap.com/lens-studio/features/try-on/garment-transfer)), but only inside Snap's tools and under Snap's patent on real-time upper-body garment exchange ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11880947)). The practical route for you is a hand-built 2D warp: cut out a photo of the garment, stretch it over your torso and limb segments using the pose keypoints, and hide the parts behind your arms using the segmentation mask. It follows you at full frame rate but looks like a sticker under strain, breaks on side views and crossed arms, and, with only a front photo, has nothing to show when you turn around. From camera to screen this pipeline should take around 50–130 ms (est., not measured); film your waving hand and the screen together in slow motion to check yours.

One live effect is already photoreal, and it matches your colour-combination use case exactly: recolouring what you are wearing. Uniqlo's 2012 mirror did this for one jacket ([UrbanDaddy](https://www.urbandaddy.com/articles/20328/san-francisco/uniqlo-qlo-in-the-dark-a-japanese-megastore-lands-in-union-square)). With MediaPipe's clothes mask split at the hip line using the pose keypoints, the mirror can tint a light-coloured kurta or pair of trousers live while keeping their real folds and shadows, so you can test colour pairings without any generative model (an untested design idea; expect imperfect mask edges).

Realistic pictures of you in a garment you are not wearing come from generative image try-on, which takes a photo of you plus a photo of the garment and returns you wearing it, in seconds rather than frames. FASHN's API charges **US$0.075 per image** on demand and US$0.06 on its higher tier ([FASHN pricing](https://help.fashn.ai/plans-and-pricing/api-pricing)) and takes 5–17 seconds depending on quality mode ([FASHN docs](https://docs.fashn.ai/api-reference/tryon-v1-6)); Google's Vertex AI Virtual Try-On costs about US$0.06 per image according to third-party trackers rather than Google's own page ([CloudPrice](https://cloudprice.net/models/google-virtual-try-on-1)); Kling's Kolors model costs US$0.07 on fal.ai ([fal.ai](https://fal.ai/models/fal-ai/kling/v1-5/kolors-virtual-try-on/llms.txt)); and the open Leffa model makes an image in about 6 seconds on an A100 ([Leffa](https://github.com/franciszzj/Leffa)). Quality is good but unreliable. Google's Doppl struggled to put trousers onto a photo of a user wearing shorts and swapped mismatched socks for AI-generated feet ([ETCentric](https://www.etcentric.org/google-doppl-lets-you-try-on-outfits-using-generative-video/)), Google warns that its own try-on can get body shape and clothing details wrong ([Google Merchant Center Help](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/16159685?hl=en)), and Alta users report low-rise jeans rendered as high-rise ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/alta-daily-digital-ai-closet/id6481705400?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). Your use case is the kinder one: the clothes are yours and already fit, so the mirror only has to show colour and combination, not predict fit.

Live photoreal video exists only in research and in costly shop pilots. LiveVVT, a paper posted on 28 August 2026, claims video try-on at 22.39 fps on undisclosed hardware ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2608.26714)); StreamDiffusionV2 needs four H100 GPUs for 58–64 fps and restyles video by prompt rather than transferring a specific garment ([StreamDiffusionV2](https://streamdiffusionv2.github.io/)). At Decart's reported US$0.05 per second, a figure from a single third-party analysis ([Genlook](https://genlook.app/blog/decart-ai-lucy-2-realtime-virtual-try-on)), ten minutes of live try-on a day would cost about **US$900 a month, roughly ₹79,000** (est.). The honest design therefore has two tiers, a live preview on the mirror and a photoreal still on request:

```
camera (rotated 90°) -> mirror board: flip + half-scale -> pose + clothes mask -> 2D warp of your garment -> screen ("preview", 30 fps)
                                              |
                                  you hold a pose for 2 s
                                              v
                         cloud try-on model -> photoreal still of you in it, 5-20 s later
```

Licences are where self-taught builders get caught, and these facts were read directly from each project's own files. The best-known open try-on models, IDM-VTON, OOTDiffusion, CatVTON, StableVITON and FitDiT, are CC BY-NC-SA, which bars commercial use ([IDM-VTON](https://github.com/yisol/IDM-VTON); [CatVTON](https://github.com/Zheng-Chong/CatVTON)), and anything built on FLUX.1-dev weights, such as OmniTry and CatVTON-FLUX, falls under a non-commercial licence ([Black Forest Labs](https://github.com/black-forest-labs/flux)). The commercially usable options are FASHN VTON v1.5 (Apache-2.0, about 2 GB of weights, accepts flat-lay garment photos), whose bundled human parser carries NVIDIA's non-commercial SegFormer licence and must be replaced ([FASHN VTON](https://github.com/fashn-AI/fashn-vton-1.5); [fashn-human-parser](https://github.com/fashn-AI/fashn-human-parser)); Leffa, whose code is MIT ([Leffa](https://github.com/franciszzj/Leffa)); and general image editors you would fine-tune for try-on, FLUX.2 [klein] 4B (Apache-2.0, about 8 GB of VRAM) ([FLUX.2](https://github.com/black-forest-labs/flux2)) and Qwen-Image-Edit (Apache-2.0) ([Qwen-Image](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen-Image)). Three more traps sit in common tools: the default background-removal model in `rembg`, BRIA RMBG-2.0, needs a paid commercial agreement ([rembg](https://github.com/danielgatis/rembg)); Ultralytics YOLO is AGPL-3.0 ([Ultralytics licence](https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/blob/main/LICENSE)); and Robust Video Matting is GPL-3.0 ([RVM](https://github.com/PeterL1n/RobustVideoMatting)). MediaPipe, RTMPose, SAM 2 and BiRefNet are permissively licensed ([SAM 2](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sam2); [BiRefNet](https://github.com/ZhengPeng7/BiRefNet)).

The wardrobe list is a product in itself. Getting clothes into an app is the category's biggest friction; Indyx charges **US$295** for a person to photograph and catalogue 100 items, though other data on its own site shows US$195 ([Indyx](https://www.myindyx.com/how-it-works)). A workable pipeline photographs each garment flat or on a hanger against a plain background, cuts it out with BiRefNet or SAM 2, extracts its main colours, and tags category, pattern and formality with FashionCLIP (MIT code) or a vision-language model, with the user confirming each tag ([FashionCLIP](https://github.com/patrickjohncyh/fashion-clip)). Home photos are harder than the studio shots most try-on models learn from, which MagicTryOn's authors acknowledge when they invite in-the-wild training data ([MagicTryOn](https://github.com/vivoCameraResearch/Magic-TryOn)). Outfit suggestions can combine occasion, dress-code and weather rules, colour-harmony scores, and compatibility models trained on the public Polyvore outfit data, whose code is BSD-licensed ([fashion-compatibility](https://github.com/mvasil/fashion-compatibility)), with a language model explaining each pick. Copying an influencer look already exists in apps: Wardrobe.AI matches a Pinterest or social-media photo to items you own ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.chicai.closet)). Chaining look matching, closet matching and try-on on your own body inside one mirror is what nobody offers.

## Off-the-shelf parts cover every hardware subsystem

Nothing in the mirror needs to be invented; the work is choosing parts and making them cooperate. The table summarises the choices, and the paragraphs below give the evidence.

| Subsystem | Prototype pick | Product pick | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screen | 55" 4K consumer TV in portrait, game mode on | 500-nit-plus, portrait-rated panel or open-cell module | TVs are not rated for portrait use |
| Glass | ~1.8 × 0.75 m MirroView 50/50 or 40/60 dielectric, 6 mm safety glass | Same, laminated | No Indian price found for display-grade glass |

| Power | Mains through certified supplies; no battery | Same, Class II | ~120–185 W while running (est.) |
| Enclosure | Aluminium-extrusion or plywood frame, vented, wall cleat | Designed enclosure | ~35–50 kg assembled (est.) |

**Screen.** A 55-inch 4K consumer TV is by far the cheapest panel: Indian listings in June–July 2026 ran from ₹26,699 (Blaupunkt) to ₹33,999 (Vu GloQLED and NU QLED) ([MySmartPrice](https://www.mysmartprice.com/electronics/pricelist/50-inch-led-tv-price-list-in-india.html); [MagicDrop](https://magicdrop.in/drops/55-inch-qled-tv)), and 43-inch sets started at ₹16,499 ([Smartprix](https://www.smartprix.com/tvs/kenstar-brand)). No TV maker's guidance on portrait mounting or its heat effects was found, so you mount at your own risk, and you should switch on game mode to cut the TV's processing delay. A commercial panel built for this job, such as Samsung's QM55C (500 nits, rated for 24/7 use in portrait), costs US$1,260 in the US but **₹2,41,105** through an Indian importer ([CrownTV](https://crowntv-us.com/blog/samsung-qm55c/); [CompSource](https://www.compsource.com/buy/QM55C/389); [Tanotis](https://www.tanotis.com/collections/commercial-monitors-displays/products/samsung-qm55c-55-uhd-4k-commercial-monitor)), about 2.2 times the US street price (est.), a reminder that one-off imports are expensive in India. Transparent OLED does not help: LG's 55-inch units list at roughly US$16,000–37,000 with 38–43% transparency and Full HD resolution ([commercialdisplay.sg](https://commercialdisplay.sg/digital-signage/lg-55ew5g/); [Creation Networks](https://creationnetworks.net/products/lg-ew5pg-s-55-class-full-hd-transparent-oled-signage-display); [Almo Pro AV](https://www.almoproav.com/productdetails/LGPRO/55EW5Q-C)), and they show the wall behind them while still only adding light.

**Glass.** No price for MirroView was found anywhere, in India or abroad. IndiaMART lists reflective and "one-way" glass at ₹75–2,900 per square foot ([IndiaMART](https://m.indiamart.com/impcat/reflective-glass.html)), which works out to roughly ₹1,100–42,000 for a 14.5 sq ft full-length panel (est.), but the cheap end is ordinary solar-control glass with unknown reflection and transmission; ask any supplier for an R/T datasheet and a sample before you buy. Acrylic two-way sheet is lighter and shatter-resistant but "less rigid, which can distort in large sheets" ([TwoWayMirrors](https://www.twowaymirrors.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/acrylic-two-way-mirror.pdf)), so keep it for a small test window. At 6 mm, glass weighs about 15 kg per square metre, so the full-length panel alone is about 20 kg and the finished mirror with a 55-inch TV and frame roughly 35–50 kg (est.).

**Camera.** The Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 Wide (102° × 67°, about US$32–35) covers a full body at 1.5 m with room to spare ([Core Electronics](https://core-electronics.com.au/raspberry-pi-camera-v3-wide-angle.html.md); [Elecena](https://elecena.pl/product/27036821/rss)), but it is a Pi camera and may need third-party drivers on a Jetson (unverified), so a USB webcam is the safer prototype choice. The Logitech C920's fixed 78° diagonal view only just frames a full body at 1.5 m when rotated (est.), and the C920s costs ₹6,999–7,999 in India ([GetPC](https://getpc.co.in/parts/webcam/logitech-c920s)); the Brio switches to a 90° view ([Synced.sg](https://synced.sg/blogs/news/logitech-brio-vs-c920)) and costs about US$168–200 ([TheTechShowdown](https://thetechshowdown.com/compare/logitech-brio-4k/logitech-c920-hd-pro)). A depth camera is optional and earns its cost only if you want body measurements or cleaner occlusion: the Orbbec Gemini 335 costs US$264 and works best between 0.26 and 3 m, which matches a dressing zone ([Orbbec store](https://store.orbbec.com/products/gemini-335); [Orbbec](https://orbbec.com/products/stereo-vision-camera/gemini-335)), while the Orbbec Femto Bolt costs ₹69,920 in India ([Tanotis](https://www.tanotis.com/collections/3d-printers-accessories/products/orbbec-femto-bolt-depth-camera)).

**Compute.** NVIDIA's Jetson Orin Nano Super developer kit launched in December 2024 at **US$249**, rated 67 TOPS (sparse) in a 25 W mode ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/NVIDIA-Jetson-Orin-Nano-Super); [Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/nvidia-launches-new-usd249-ai-development-board-that-does-67-tops)), and ThinkRobotics lists it at ₹29,999–32,499 in undated listings ([ThinkRobotics](https://thinkrobotics.com/collections/buy-nvidia-jetson-developer-kits-online)). In July 2026 NVIDIA raised Jetson prices by up to 101%, taking the 8 GB Orin Nano module from US$299 to US$399 in 1,000-unit lots ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2026/07/22/nvidia-increases-the-price-of-jetson-modules-and-devkits-by-up-to-101/)), so re-check the price on the day you buy. Cheaper boards handle tracking but little else: a Raspberry Pi 5 with the 26-TOPS AI HAT+ (US$110) or the 40-TOPS AI HAT+ 2 with its own 8 GB (US$130) ([Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/raspberry-pi/raspberry-pi-release-higher-performance-ai-hat-13-and-26-tops-variants); [Tecnoblog](https://tecnoblog.net/noticias/raspberry-pi-anuncia-placa-ai-hat-2-com-40-tops-para-ia-preco-e-alto/)), though the 16 GB Pi 5 itself rose to US$205 in February 2026 in the memory shortage ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/raspberry-pi/raspberry-pi-5-price-increases-drastically-as-ai-shortage-bites-16gb-version-now-usd205-second-price-increase-in-three-months-over-70-percent-more-expensive-than-original-msrp)); or an RK3588 board such as the Radxa Rock 5B 8 GB at about ₹12,179 including GST ([Hubtronics](https://hubtronics.in/radxa/rs119-d8?page=5)). None of them runs generative try-on. CatVTON needs about 8 GB of VRAM at 1024 × 768 ([CatVTON](https://github.com/Zheng-Chong/CatVTON)), OmniTry at least 28 GB ([OmniTry](https://github.com/Kunbyte-AI/OmniTry)) and CatVTON-FLUX 40 GB or more ([catvton-flux](https://github.com/nftblackmagic/catvton-flux)), which means a desktop graphics card (an RTX 5060 sold from ₹36,999 in July 2026 ([GPUPrix](https://gpuprix.com/zh/india/gpus/geforce-rtx-5060))) or the cloud.

**Touch and control.** A 55-inch IR touch frame costs US$51–164 on Alibaba in lots of ten, US$160 on eBay and US$298 on Newegg ([Alibaba](https://electronics.alibaba.com/product/ir-touch-screen-frame-overlay); [eBay](https://www.ebay.com/str/professionaltouchscreenstore); [Newegg](https://www.newegg.com/p/3C6-05G2-01AA3)). It senses a finger through a light curtain just above the glass, so glass thickness does not matter, but it sits as a raised frame on the front and breaks the flush mirror look. A PCAP foil bonded behind the glass is invisible and works through up to 10 mm (multi-user) or 20 mm (single-user) of glass ([Pilkington](https://my.pilkington.com/en/news-insights/latest/nsg-group-and-visualplanet-to-exhibit-integrated-smart-mirror-at-ise-2017); [Installation International](https://www.installation-international.com/ise-daily/single-user-touchfoil-rugged-environments)), but it costs **US$1,976.87** for 55 inches at retail ([TwoWayMirrors](https://www.twowaymirrors.com/pcap-foil/)), and metallic mirror coatings can block it (unverified; ask the foil vendor). Touch also has a usability problem: you browse the wardrobe list at arm's length, but you try clothes on standing 1.5–2 m back, out of reach. Use touch for browsing, and switch outfits mid-try-on with a paired phone (LOOOK.AI pairs by QR code), simple gestures seen by the camera, or voice.

**Lighting and colour.** Because the whole point is judging colour combinations, lighting and calibration matter more here than in any information mirror. Use diffused, dimmable 24 V LED strips with a high colour-rendering index on both sides of the glass (the research could not price them, so the cost table uses placeholders). Then photograph a colour-checker card under the mirror's own lights to build a colour correction for the camera, and lock the display's picture mode and white point. Without both steps the digital mirror shifts garment colours, which defeats its purpose.

**Power and charging.** There is nothing to charge. A 55-inch TV and Jetson build draws about 120–185 W while running (est.), built from a 55-inch 4K set's 70–90 W or more ([Webwoordenboek](https://webwoordenboek.nl/kenniscentrum/hoeveel-watt-is-een-4k-tv)), the Jetson's 25 W top mode, and assumed loads for lights and fans; a 43-inch TV draws about 54 W ([BT energy fiche](https://m.businessdirect.bt.com/images/EnergyComplianceInfo/FY5V/EnergyFicheURL/631257_fiche-27924.pdf)). Three hours of use takes 360–555 Wh (est.), which would need a 0.5–1 kWh battery station: weight, cost, a lithium fire risk in a bedroom, and extra certification, since India already treats power banks as a compulsory BIS registration category ([eChai](https://echai.ventures/d2c/d2c-product-licenses/bis-crs-mark-electronics)), all for no benefit in a room with a socket. Run on mains, sleep the screen and lights with a presence sensor, and make the software save its state so power cuts do no harm.

**Enclosure.** Build the frame from aluminium extrusion or plywood with a rear service panel that opens only with a tool, vents at the bottom and top, one or two quiet fans, and the compute board outside the TV's hot exhaust. At 35–50 kg the mirror must be screwed to the wall with a cleat or VESA mount and anchors suited to brick or RCC; a freestanding version needs a weighted base and an anti-tip strap. Because the glass has no solid backing over the screen area, use laminated glass, or toughened glass with a clear safety film on the back, so a break does not drop shards (glass safety standards were not checked in this research).

## Shock safety comes from the power architecture, not from care

The standard that fits a display-plus-computer-plus-camera product is IEC 62368-1, and India has just moved to it. A MeitY notification of 7 November 2025 brought **IS/IEC 62368-1:2023** into BIS's Compulsory Registration Scheme (CRS), replacing IS 13252 (Part 1):2010 and IS 616:2017, which may still be used until 1 November 2028 ([SGS](https://www.sgs.com/en-no/news/2026/04/gma-update-april); [Matrix Test Labs](https://matrixtestlabs.com/blog/meity-announces-migration-to-isiec-62368-12023-a-major-shift-for-electronics-it-product-compliance)). Design to the 2023 edition from day one. The standard sorts every energy source into classes and lets ordinary people touch only class 1, "ES1": no more than **60 V DC or 30 V rms**, or a touch current no more than **2 mA DC or 0.5 mA rms** ([Delta Electronics](https://psu.deltaww.com/de/industry-know-how/what-is-the-difference-between-selv-pelv-and-es1-in-ac-dc-power-supplies)). It also requires that a single fault can raise a touchable part no higher than ES2 ([Element](https://www.element.com/resources/articles/en-62368-standard-update-information)), so the barrier between mains and the low-voltage side must survive one failure, which is the job of reinforced or double insulation inside a certified power supply. "Can never get a shock" therefore means that even after one component fails, nothing a hand can reach carries hazardous voltage. Warnings and careful users do not deliver that; the layout of the power system does.

| Rule | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Keep 230 V out of the mirror | Feed it from a certified external Class II adaptor, or put one certified power supply in a sealed metal or flame-rated box that opens only with a tool, with a fused IEC inlet, strain relief and a warning label | The mirror becomes an ES1-only product; adaptors are a compulsory BIS category, so buy registered ones ([Seyecs](https://blogs.seyecs.com/bis-certification-for-electronic-products/)) |
| Run everything touchable at 24 V DC or less | LEDs, camera, compute, touch frame, speakers and sensors; no 230 V LED strips | 24 V sits far below the 60 V DC ES1 limit ([Delta Electronics](https://psu.deltaww.com/de/industry-know-how/what-is-the-difference-between-selv-pelv-and-es1-in-ac-dc-power-supplies)) |
| Let users touch only glass | Sense touch with an IR frame or a PCAP foil behind the glass | Glass is an insulator, so fingers never meet an electrode ([Pilkington](https://my.pilkington.com/en/news-insights/latest/nsg-group-and-visualplanet-to-exhibit-integrated-smart-mirror-at-ise-2017)) |
| Prefer Class II (double insulation) | Do not rely on the household earth; if you use an earthed supply with a metal frame, bond every touchable metal part to earth | Earthing quality varies from home to home |
| Measure leakage on the whole product | Test the finished unit, because the small leakage currents of several switch-mode supplies add together | The 0.5 mA rms ES1 limit applies to what a person can touch, not to each supply alone ([Delta Electronics](https://psu.deltaww.com/de/industry-know-how/what-is-the-difference-between-selv-pelv-and-es1-in-ac-dc-power-supplies)) |
| Fuse each low-voltage branch | Current-limit or fuse each DC branch separately | Once shock is designed out, fire is the main hazard left on the low-voltage side |
| Keep the glass cool | Vent hot parts away from the glass; throttle and shut down on over-temperature | The standard caps the touch temperature of accessible surfaces, based on ISO 13732-1 ([UL](https://japan.ul.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/27/2014/06/1_techbrief_touchtemp.pdf)) |
| Plan for cleaning spray | Drip edge at the bottom, electronics above it, gasketed touch frame; label it "not for bathrooms" | People spray glass cleaner on mirrors |

The TV-based prototype needs one adaptation. A consumer TV takes 230 V directly, but its mains section already sits inside its own certified casing, so leave the TV closed, route its cord and every adaptor into one closed rear compartment that needs a screwdriver to open, and keep every connection you make yourself on the low-voltage side; never splice mains cable or use open terminal blocks. Treat a household 30 mA RCCB as a backup, not as your design. Schneider describes high-sensitivity RCDs as "additional" protection precisely because the other measures "cannot be regarded as being infallible" ([Schneider Electric](https://download.schneider-electric.com/doc/CA908066E/CA908066E%20%28web%29.pdf)), and one maker specifies tripping within 30 ms and a monthly test ([Gira](https://download.gira.com/data3/01141190.pdf)). Your manual should recommend an RCCB-protected socket and a surge protector, while the mirror stays safe on a socket that has neither.

Certification is less settled than the engineering. "Smart mirror" is not a named category in the CRS lists, which vendor guides count at about 74–75 categories covering monitors, display units, LED lights and power adapters ([Seyecs](https://blogs.seyecs.com/bis-certification-for-electronic-products/); [eChai](https://echai.ventures/d2c/d2c-product-licenses/bis-crs-mark-electronics)), and BIS had to issue explicit guidance before smart and AR glasses were placed, each needing its own registration ([CliniExperts](https://cliniexperts.com/regulatory-update/guidance-on-product-category-under-compulsory-registration-scheme-crs/)). Whether your mirror needs registration as a whole therefore depends on how BIS classifies it, so ask BIS or MeitY in writing before launch and use CRS-registered adaptors and LED drivers in the meantime. Registered products are tested at BIS-recognised labs to IS/IEC 62368-1:2023 ([SGS](https://www.sgs.com/en-no/news/2026/04/gma-update-april)), and that test report is your best evidence of safety whatever the classification turns out to be. The research also flagged wireless approval for Wi-Fi or Bluetooth and e-waste EPR registration as likely requirements, but could not verify either and found no Indian fee or timeline for any of these steps.

## Demand, privacy and colour fidelity are the real risks

### Four comparable devices shut down, and phone try-on is free

Every close analogue failed or retreated. Amazon's Echo Look, a US$199.99 bedroom camera that judged your outfits, lasted about three years before Amazon moved its style features to the Shopping app ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/05/29/echo-looks-will-cease-functioning-in-july-as-amazon-discontinues-the-camera)); one reviewer called its advice "tone-deaf" ([TechHive](https://www.techhive.com/article/578600/amazon-s-fashion-centric-echo-look-camera-will-reportedly-stop-working-in-july.html)). Lululemon paid **US$500 million** for the MIRROR home fitness mirror in 2020, cut its US$1,495 launch price to US$995 ([The Bridge](https://thebridge.jp/2018/09/mirror-raises-25-million-and-launches-1495-connected-mirror-for-virtual-fitness-classes); [RetailWire](https://retailwire.com/discussion/what-does-lululemon-see-when-it-looks-in-its-mirror/)), recorded a US$407.9 million impairment in fiscal 2022 and US$98.2 million more in pre-tax charges in fiscal 2023, roughly the whole purchase price, and stopped selling the hardware in December 2023 ([Lululemon 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1397187/000139718724000010/lulu-20240128.htm); [Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/lululemon-discontinues-mirror-peloton-apparel-fitness-content-partnership/694995/)). Naked Labs' US$1,395 body-scanning mirror ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/08/01/naked-labs-raises-14m-series-a-led-by-founders-fund-for-its-3d-body-scanning-mirror)) ended its service on 31 October 2023, leaving owners with a useless device ([Naked Labs](https://nakedlabs.com/)), and Google closed Doppl after ten months ([Google Labs Help](https://support.google.com/labs/answer/16537062?hl=en)). The pattern is a high upfront price, a narrow use, and features that slide into a phone app, which is exactly where Google's free try-on in India already sits ([Google India blog](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/virtual-apparel-try-on-tool-comes-to-india/)). Naked Labs adds a rule for anyone selling a ₹1 lakh device: it must keep working offline if your company disappears.

The evidence behind "young people spend an hour trying outfits" is thin. No Indian survey of time spent choosing clothes was found. The only time polls are British, commissioned by retailers or a uniform supplier in 2015–16, and cover women only: about 17 minutes each morning in a Marks & Spencer poll ([Hello Magazine](https://www.hellomagazine.com/fashion/news/451466/women-six-months-deciding-what-wear/)), and about 12 minutes per workday but **27.5 minutes before a night out** in another ([TODAY](https://www.today.com/today/amp/tdna30766)). An hour fits occasions such as weddings, festivals, dates and interviews rather than daily dressing, so pitch occasion and dress-code planning rather than time saved. "Decision fatigue" is weak ground too: a meta-analysis of 50 choice-overload experiments found an average effect of "virtually zero" ([Scheibehenne et al.](https://abcwest.cogs.indiana.edu/pmwiki/pdf/scheibehenne.options.2010.pdf)). Better-supported hooks are social visibility, since 43% of 18–35-year-olds in a 2026 German survey avoid repeating outfits in posts ([FashionUnited](https://fashionunited.uk/news/fashion/h-m-2026-study-fashion-between-self-expression-and-social-pressure/2026052288218)), and unworn clothes, since one in four respondents in an Indian wardrobe study use no more than 40% of their wardrobe ([OsloMet](https://clothingresearch.oslomet.no/2024/02/27/wardrobe-size-volume-of-clothing-and-shopping-practices-in-india/)).

Who pays is the harder question. Snap and BCG estimate Indian Gen Z spending at about US$860 billion, but about **US$660 billion of that is household spending they influence** rather than money they earn ([Snap Newsroom](https://newsroom.snap.com/how-gen-z-is-shaping-the-new-india?lang=en-US)), so parents often pay for a ₹1 lakh device. Influencers are the top purchase driver for only **6%** of Gen Z in a Business Today–PRICE survey of 4,311 people, against 43% for product quality, even though Instagram is the most influential platform at 44% ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/magazine/editors-note/story/the-gen-z-consumer-beyond-cliches-512292-2026-01-22)). Retailers pay for try-on mainly to cut returns, which run 25–40% in Indian online apparel ([Apparel Resources](https://apparelresources.com/business-news/retail/brands-make-returns-free-dont/)), but a mirror for clothes you already own does nothing for returns, so retailers have no reason to subsidise it unless you add "try before you buy". The more credible early payer is the occasion-wear showroom. India's wedding industry is about US$130 billion by a Jefferies estimate ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/biz-tech/2024/06/25/weddings-now-a-dollar130-billion-industry-expenses-only-seen-going-up-disproportionately-in-most-cases.amp.html)); women's ethnic wear was projected at ₹1.68 trillion for FY2025, with 72% sold through unorganised retail ([Statista](https://es.statista.com/statistics/1328065/india-women-s-ethnic-wear-market-size-by-segment/)); chains including Chennai Silks, Shoppers Stop and Lifestyle have tried digital trial rooms ([Apparel Resources](https://apparelresources.com/business-news/retail/apparel-brands-upping-experience-quotient/)); and Indian vendors already list virtual dressing mirrors at **₹65,000–₹7.5 lakh** ([IndiaMART](https://www.indiamart.com/proddetail/virtual-dressing-room-13927869973.html); [IndiaMART](https://www.indiamart.com/proddetail/virtual-dressing-room-mirror-20004707512.html)). The catch is technical: sarees, dupattas and lehengas are the hardest garments for generative try-on.

### India's data-protection duties start in May 2027

A camera in a room where people change clothes is the product's biggest trust problem. India notified the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules on **13 November 2025** ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014&reg=3&lang=2)); consent-manager provisions apply about 12 months later, in November 2026, and most obligations, including notice, consent, security safeguards, breach reporting and user rights, from about **May 2027** ([EY India](https://www.ey.com/en_in/insights/cybersecurity/transforming-data-privacy-digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025); [Privy by IDfy](https://www.privybyidfy.com/blog/dpdp-compliance-guide-2026-what-indian-enterprises-must-do-before-may-2027)), with breaches reported within 72 hours ([Cy5](https://www.cy5.io/blog/dpdp-rules-2025-complete-compliance-guide-cloud-security/)). A product launching in 2027 must comply from its first day. The defensible design runs the live camera loop entirely on the mirror, holds frames in memory and never stores them by default, fits a physical lens shutter and a switch that cuts camera power, wires the camera LED to the camera's own power so software cannot record without the light, sends a photo to the cloud renderer only after explicit opt-in, deletes it soon after rendering, and never uses customer images for model training or human labelling. Choose users by profile or PIN rather than face recognition, which is biometric processing. Keeping frames on the device also shrinks the personal data your company holds at all. If you target teenagers, check DPDP's rules on children before launch; the Act is understood to treat under-18s as children who need verifiable parental consent, a point this research could not verify against the Act's text.

### Colour accuracy is the promise and the weak point

The product's core question, "does this colour combination work?", depends on the weakest link in a digital mirror. The screen shows what the camera saw, after the camera's white balance and the display's picture processing, under whatever light is in the room; without the colour-checker calibration and locked display settings described above, the mirror misjudges the one thing it exists to judge. The optical conflicts stack up: bright front light for colour judgement brightens the reflection the screen must overpower, high-transmission glass makes a dimmer mirror, a top camera distorts perspective, and TV processing adds delay. Generative quality adds its own failures, because home photos of folded or hanging clothes differ from the studio images models learn from, and draped Indian garments are harder still; Google's try-on reportedly skips saree and lehenga listings (vendor claim) ([Ai Vastra](https://aivastra.com/how-google-virtual-try-on-is-changing-fashion-shopping-in-india/)). Test a saree and a dupatta in the first week of software work, because that result decides whether Indian occasion wear is your advantage or your wall.

### Two patent families need clearing before launch

US 11,602,232, continued as US 12,426,728 and assigned to ACI Holdings according to Google Patents, covers smart mirrors that generate and display clothing recommendations, with 2019 priority and active status ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12426728)). Snap holds a granted patent on real-time upper-body garment exchange ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11880947)) and 2024–25 filings on applying a selected AR fashion item to a person in a live camera feed ([Nweon](https://patent.nweon.com/37783)). Amazon's 2018 blended-reality mirror patent shows as "Expired – Fee Related" on Google Patents, an automated status rather than a legal finding ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9858719)). None of the claims were read, so get a freedom-to-operate opinion before raising money or selling. Fast followers are the other competitive threat: EONEOMS already has the home wardrobe mirror, LOOOK.AI the live renderer and Qualcomm an on-device reference design, and Whering or Alta could add a camera "mirror mode" to a tablet on a stand without building any hardware.

## Build the software first and the glass last

The most feasible version 1 is a full-length frame holding about 1.8 × 0.75 m of 6 mm MirroView-50/50-class safety glass, with a 55-inch screen in portrait behind its upper two-thirds; a wide USB camera rotated 90° on the top bezel; a Jetson Orin Nano Super running the flip, scaling, pose, clothes segmentation and a 2D warp of your own garment photos at 30 fps; dimmable 24 V side lights; an IR touch frame around the screen zone for browsing your wardrobe; and a paired phone for switching outfits from a distance. Holding a pose for two seconds sends one frame, with your consent, to a try-on API, and a photoreal still of you in that garment appears 5–20 seconds later beside the live preview. Mains power stays inside certified supplies in a tool-only compartment, and everything else runs at 24 V or less. Each stage below exists to kill the idea cheaply if it is going to die; the survey sizes follow the market research's suggestion, and the pass marks are this report's suggestions, not sourced benchmarks.

Stage 1 answers the riskiest technical question, whether a live preview plus a photoreal snapshot of your own clothes feels useful, for under ₹10,000. Stage 2 answers the riskiest market question, whether people keep a digital wardrobe up to date, for the price of your time. Buy glass only after both pass.

## Conclusion

The research changes what the mirror is for. Its unique asset is not the display, which is a commodity TV, but the camera at the moment of dressing: it captures a standard, full-length, evenly lit image of you every day, which is exactly the input that phone try-on lacks, and it can log what you actually wear, turning the cataloguing chore that a US service charges US$295 per 100 items to do into a by-product of getting dressed. Build the software around that data — your clothes, your body, Indian occasions and dress codes — and the mirror becomes one front end among several, alongside a phone app and a showroom unit where a shopkeeper, not a parent, pays for the hardware.

"Real time" will arrive through price rather than invention. Live photoreal try-on already runs in shops at about US$180 per hour of use; fitting ten minutes a day into a ₹500 monthly budget needs that cost to fall roughly 150-fold, to about US$1 an hour (est.), and claims such as LiveVVT's 22 fps show the research heading that way. Design version 1 so the photoreal tier is a swappable cloud service behind a good camera, a reliable network link and an honest "preview" label on the live view, and the mirror can move from stills to live video through a software update when the price falls, without new glass.
