Research brief · October 2026 · revised for the sticker idea

AI Try-On Mirror Sticker

An add-on you stick onto the mirror you already own. It makes the mirror respond to touch, lists the clothes you own, and shows you wearing any of them as you move. This brief checks whether it exists, what a sticker can physically do, which parts you need, how to keep it shock-safe, and what it costs.

Revised: the first version researched a complete smart mirror. You clarified that people want an overlay for the mirror they already have, so the research was redone for that. The full-mirror findings are kept near the end for comparison.

Already exists?
No. Nothing on sale adds a screen and camera to an existing mirror for outfit try-on, and no crowdfunding for one was found from 2024–26. The nearest things are magnetic or suction phone mounts for mirrors, touch panels sold to shops, and the try-on mirrors and phone apps in the market scan below.
Can a sticker do it?
Half of it. A thin film or frame can make your mirror touch-sensitive. No film can show you wearing the clothes: see-through LED film is coarse, can only add light, and sits on the glass while your reflection sits behind it. What works is a slim screen and a camera mounted over part of the mirror.
Recommended kit
A magnetic dock stuck to the mirror, holding a budget 11-inch tablet that is the screen, camera and computer in one: about ₹12,000–17,700. A dock for the user's own phone or tablet costs ₹499–1,499.
How real it looks
The live view is fast but approximate. A photo-real look takes 5–20 seconds through a cloud model at about ₹6–7 each. On an 11-inch tablet you appear about a quarter of your reflection's size.
Safe to touch?
Yes. Only a certified USB-C adapter at the socket carries 230 V, and nothing on the mirror runs above 20 V. The bigger risk is a fall: keep anything held to glass by tape under about 1.3 kg and add a safety strap.
Biggest risks
A camera where people change clothes, free phone try-on from Google and others, and whether people keep their digital wardrobe up to date.

Your idea

A sticker for the mirror you already own

The product, as I now understand it:

  • An add-on that sticks onto a mirror people already have: a bedroom wall mirror, an almirah door or a dressing table. Nobody has to buy a new mirror.
  • It makes the mirror a touchscreen, so you can tap through the clothes you own.
  • A small camera sees you, and AI shows you wearing the selected outfit and follows your movement.
  • It suggests colour combinations and dress-code-appropriate outfits, including influencer-style looks rebuilt from your own clothes.

The problem holds up, but the numbers are smaller than an hour a day. The only time-use surveys found are UK polls of women from 2015–16: about 12–17 minutes choosing an outfit on a weekday morning and about 27 minutes before a night out. No Indian survey was found. The hour-long sessions are most likely the high-stakes moments: weddings, festivals, nights out, interviews.

Two related pains have better evidence. Clothes go unworn: an OsloMet study in India found 1 in 4 people use no more than 40% of their wardrobe. And people avoid repeating outfits seen online: 43% of 18–35-year-olds did in a 2026 German survey. Among Indian Gen Z shoppers, Instagram is the most influential platform (44%), though only 6% say influencers are their main reason to buy.

Camera lens

A reflection is always half your height on the glass, because it appears twice as far away as the glass itself. The size on screen allows 8% head and foot room; screens are portrait 16:9 except the 19.5:9 phone and 16:10 tablet. Eye height is taken as 93.6% of body height.

Can a sticker do it?

A sticker can add touch, but not the picture

Three jobs have to happen on the mirror: sense your finger, see you, and show you in the new outfit. Thin films and frames handle the first job. A small camera handles the second. The third is where every sticker-like overlay fails, for two reasons that come from optics, not from today's technology.

Does it already exist?

Nobody sells a try-on sticker

Nothing found as of October 2026 adds a screen and a camera to a mirror you already own for outfit try-on, and no crowdfunding for one turned up from 2024–26. The parts exist separately, mostly for fitness and shop windows. Full try-on mirrors exist only in stores, and the products that know your own clothes show a still image, an avatar or a list.

What the earlier attempts teach

  • Price sinks home hardware. MIRROR sold at US$1,495 plus a subscription and was written off within three years. A ₹499 dock and a ₹12,000–17,700 tablet kit avoid that trap; a full mirror walks into it.
  • The phone usually wins. Amazon moved Echo Look's features into its shopping app, and Google, Glance and Zara now give photo try-on away free. A mirror kit must do what a phone in your hand can't: hands-free, full-body and live, at the moment you dress.
  • Add-on cameras get dropped. Peloton Guide, a camera that turned an existing TV into a trainer, was discontinued in 2025. Reuse the camera people already own where you can.
  • A device that depends on the cloud dies with its company. Naked Labs mirrors stopped working when the service ended. Keep the core features working offline.

How it works

Three loops: wardrobe, live mirror, photo-real look

Body tracking is solved: current models run at 30 fps and faster on phones and small boards. The hard part is the garment. Live methods are fast but approximate: they warp a flat garment photo onto your body, or they need a 3D model of every garment, which no one will make for a personal wardrobe. Photo-real generative try-on works from one garment photo but takes seconds per image.

A quick win to test first: recolour the garment you are already wearing, live (clothes mask plus a colour shift), as Uniqlo's 2012 store mirror did. It looks real because the folds and fit are real, and it answers "would this shirt work in navy?" The research flags it as untested.

Mounting rules

  • Under about 1.3 kg on tape, always with a safety strap. Heavier 21.5–27″ panels (3–6.5 kg) need a hook, a cleat or a floor stand.
  • Tape goes on the front glass only. Damage to the silver comes from solvent adhesives or acid-curing silicone on the back.
  • Mind the door. Wardrobe hinges are rated for 4–6 kg doors on two hinges, so add no more than about 1 kg, and route the cable so the door can swing.
  • Leave an air gap. A screen pressed against the glass can warm it by 10–20 °C; glass can crack when one area runs about 35 °C hotter than the rest (estimate).
  • Skip bathrooms for any screen tier: humidity and splashes.

Electrical safety

Only 20 V reaches the mirror

Design rules

  • Mains stays in the adapter. Use a BIS-registered USB-C adapter; adapters and power banks are compulsory categories under India's BIS registration scheme.
  • Everything on the mirror runs at 20 V DC or less. No hand-wired mains anywhere in the kit.
  • The likelier injury is a fall, not a shock. US data puts furniture and TV tip-overs at about 17,800 injuries a year, 44% of them children. Hence the weight limit and the strap.
  • If you ever use LED film: it runs at 5 V but draws 20–60 A for half a square metre at full brightness, so connectors and wiring become a fire risk. Use rated wiring, fuses and a certified supply.
  • Batteries: the tablet's own certified battery is fine. Don't add large DIY lithium packs.
  • Plug into a socket on a 30 mA RCCB where you can: extra protection for the home.

Privacy

A camera where people change clothes

This is the product's biggest trust problem. The camera faces the place people dress, and many target users are under 18. A tablet you can snap off and put away already helps; build the rest into the app and the dock.

  • Camera off by default. It starts only when you begin a try-on, and a cover or shutter is part of the dock.
  • On the device by default. The live view never leaves the tablet. An HD look sends one photo to a cloud API only after you opt in, and only to a provider whose data-retention terms you have checked.
  • No human review and no model training on customer images. Encrypt stored photos and offer one-tap delete.
  • Core features work offline, so the kit keeps working if the company or its cloud service disappears.

Running costs

  • Live view: nothing per use, because it runs on the tablet.
  • HD looks through an API: US$0.06–0.075 each, about ₹6–7. Five a day for a month is roughly ₹800–1,000 per active user, so plan a ₹99–199 monthly subscription or render packs (estimate).
  • Your own GPU instead: an L4 rents for US$0.59–0.97 an hour. An always-on GPU beats the API above roughly 5,700–9,400 looks a month.
  • Live photo-real video at about US$0.05 a second is far too expensive for home use today; it needs to get roughly 150 times cheaper.

Building the team

  • Salaries in India (AmbitionBox, 2025): computer-vision engineers ₹3.3–22 lakh a year (senior ₹20–45 lakh), ML engineers ₹3.5–25 lakh, embedded engineers about ₹2–16 lakh.
  • The kit needs less hardware work than a mirror: mostly an app, a dock and packaging. You on the app and front end plus one CV/ML engineer is a workable start.
  • Grants worth checking: DST NIDHI-PRAYAS (up to ₹10 lakh, aimed at prototypes), MeitY TIDE 2.0 (up to ₹7 lakh), MeitY SAMRIDH (up to ₹40 lakh), Startup India Seed Fund (₹20 lakh grant; status after 2025 unconfirmed).

Build plan

App first, dock second, glass never

The kit is mostly software, so each step costs little and can stop the idea cheaply if it isn't working.

  1. 1 · Weeks 1–2

    Check the problem

    Survey 300–500 people aged 16–30 and run a one-to-two-week dressing diary: minutes per session, outfits tried, which occasions. Move on only if long try-on sessions recur weekly.

  2. 2 · Weeks 2–5

    Laptop rig

    Webcam rotated 90°, a screen on its side, MediaPipe pose and clothes mask, live recolouring, a 2D overlay of 20 of your own garments, and FASHN API snapshots. Under ₹10,000; 100 API images cost about ₹660.

  3. 3 · Weeks 5–12

    App and ₹499 dock

    Wardrobe capture, outfit ideas by colour, dress code, weather and occasion, and HD looks, on a phone or tablet in a mirror dock. Test with 30–50 people. Move on when they come back weekly without reminders.

  4. 4 · Months 3–5

    Tablet kit

    Magnetic plate, strap, adapter and a bundled budget tablet at ₹12,000–16,000. Bench-test a capacitive touch film on a real mirror if whole-mirror touch matters.

  5. 5 · Month 5 on

    Sell and decide

    Sell to the first 50–100 users, or pilot in two to five ethnic-wear showrooms. Apply for NIDHI-PRAYAS or TIDE 2.0. Get a patent freedom-to-operate opinion before raising money.

Two patent families need checking: ACI Holdings' US 11,602,232 and US 12,426,728 (smart mirrors that display clothing recommendations) and Snap's US 11,880,947 (real-time garment exchange on live video). Their claims were not read in this research.

Full-mirror version, for comparison

The complete smart mirror researched first

Show the full-mirror findings

The first version of this brief designed a whole new mirror: two-way glass with a 43–55-inch screen behind it, a Jetson AI computer and an IR touch frame. It works, using the same flipped-video trick, but the prototype costs ₹1.07–1.69 lakh (about US$1,200–1,900 at ₹88 per US$), and the retail price would land where Lululemon's MIRROR failed.

Why the full mirror needs special glass

Behind a two-way mirror the screen has to outshine your reflection, and ordinary mirror-TV glass lets so little light through that a normal TV loses to the reflection of a white shirt. A 50/50 glass with a bright 500–700-nit panel is the minimum. A stick-on screen avoids this problem entirely, because nothing sits behind the glass.
